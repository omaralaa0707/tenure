# Tenure — Design Context

## Design Context

### Users
Founding attorneys and managing partners at US **immigration law firms** with 2–10 attorneys. They arrive from a cold email, during a working weekday, usually on a laptop between client matters. Their world is US government paperwork: I-130s, I-485s, USCIS receipt notices, court dates. Their job-to-be-done on this page is to decide in under three minutes whether an AI intake coordinator could plausibly handle the enquiries currently going cold in their inbox — and whether the company behind it is real enough to trust with client contact.

The secondary reader is the firm's paralegal or office manager, who will be asked "does this seem legitimate?"

### Brand Personality
Three words: **clerical, exacting, unhurried.**

Not "modern," not "elegant." The brand is an employment firm that happens to be new technology — it should read like a well-set employment contract, not a SaaS landing page. Institutional trust at founder speed. Calm, precise, warm-formal. Numbers instead of adjectives. No exclamation points, no emoji, no growth-marketing cadence.

The emotional goal is **relief followed by confidence** — relief that the intake problem is someone else's job now, confidence that a named human is accountable when it goes wrong.

### Aesthetic Direction — revised 2026-07-26, second revision
**A real product website that happens to sell an employment relationship**, not a document rendered in a browser. Two rounds of feedback corrected the direction here, in order:

1. First build ("too bland") tried pure restraint: hairline rules only, no scale contrast, no motion.
2. Amplification pass ("don't like it") pushed decoration instead of structure: paper-grain texture, an ink stamp, oversized ghost numerals, a full-bleed pull quote. Reverted in full.
3. Third build ("looks like a weird document with a demo chat box") diagnosed the actual problem: every section used the same label-rail-plus-content grid, every heading was set in serif, and the role scorecard and offer letter were literal `<dl>` field lists. The whole page read as one long form, not a website with a nav, a hero, and distinct sections.

**What changed structurally, and stays the rule going forward:**
- **A real nav bar exists** (sticky, wordmark, links to in-page sections, a primary CTA button). A one-page site with no nav reads as unfinished, not minimal.
- **Section layouts vary.** Hero is two-column with a visual (a labeled, static sample offer-letter card, not the label-rail pattern repeated). Feature section is copy-plus-card. Timeline is a card grid. Pricing is a card grid. FAQ is a real `<details>` accordion. Each section looks like what it is, not a recurring template.
- **Structural headings default to Public Sans, not Caslon.** Serif is now reserved for: the hero headline, the final CTA headline, and the AI candidate's own speech in the transcript. Every section header, card title, and nav label is sans. This was the single biggest fix — serif on every heading is what made the page read as a printed document rather than a site.
- **Document components (role scorecard, offer letter) are still literal artifacts, but styled as cards, not forms.** Label/value pairs live in a card with a heading and a boundary note, not a bordered `<dl>` that looks like a fillable PDF.
- Alternating section backgrounds (`--paper` / `--surface`) now do the job hairline rules alone used to do, giving the page visual rhythm a scroller can feel.

**Kept from the original direction, still true:**
- **Theme: light primary,** dark fully supported via token swap, not inversion.
- **Type pairing:** Libre Caslon reserved for voice moments; Public Sans for everything structural (expanded from "everything with a job" to "everything, full stop, except the few voice moments listed above").
- **One accent: oxblood.** Still never a gradient. Now also used as a 3px card-top accent on pricing cards and a background fill on the final CTA band, in addition to the original signature moments.
- **Anti-references unchanged:** the AI-startup house style (cream grounds, Instrument/Newsreader serifs, purple-blue gradients, glassmorphism, robot imagery, glowing orbs) and legal-industry cliché (gavels, columns, scales of justice, navy-and-gold).

### Design Principles
1. **Structure the section, don't decorate the page.** A nav, a hero, varied section layouts, a footer: those are what make something read as a website. Texture and ornament were the wrong lever both times this was corrected.
2. **One heading voice per role.** Sans for structure, serif only where a human or the candidate is actually speaking. Repeating the serif on every heading was the recurring mistake.
3. **Artifacts are content, not the layout system.** The offer letter and role scorecard are real documents inside cards. The page around them is a normal marketing site, not more paperwork.
4. **The demo is the argument.** The live interview is still the page's thesis. Everything above it gets the attorney there; everything below answers what it raised.
5. **Accountable, not self-announcing.** Revised 2026-07-26: the site no longer foregrounds "it's AI, not a person" as a selling point (no badges, no dedicated FAQ item, no unprompted line in the candidate's own opening). The AI candidate still never claims to be human and answers truthfully if asked directly, that floor stays for compliance and trust, but it's a quiet backstop now, not a marketing beat. What's still emphasized: a named human manager is accountable when it errs. No synthetic faces, no human names for AI staff, no invented statistics.
