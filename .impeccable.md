# Tenure — Design Context

## Design Context

### Users
Founding attorneys and managing partners at US **immigration law firms** with 2–10 attorneys. They arrive from a cold email, during a working weekday, usually on a laptop between client matters. Their world is US government paperwork: I-130s, I-485s, USCIS receipt notices, court dates. Their job-to-be-done on this page is to decide in under three minutes whether an AI intake coordinator could plausibly handle the enquiries currently going cold in their inbox — and whether the company behind it is real enough to trust with client contact.

The secondary reader is the firm's paralegal or office manager, who will be asked "does this seem legitimate?"

### Brand Personality
Three words: **clerical, exacting, unhurried.**

Not "modern," not "elegant." The brand is an employment firm that happens to be new technology — it should read like a well-set employment contract, not a SaaS landing page. Institutional trust at founder speed. Calm, precise, warm-formal. Numbers instead of adjectives. No exclamation points, no emoji, no growth-marketing cadence.

The emotional goal is **relief followed by confidence** — relief that the intake problem is someone else's job now, confidence that a named human is accountable when it goes wrong.

### Aesthetic Direction
**Editorial-institutional.** The governing metaphor: *the identity is the paperwork.* Offer letters, role scorecards, personnel files, standup reports. Every surface should feel like a document that was typeset rather than a screen that was designed.

- **Theme: light primary.** Derived from context, not habit — this is a daytime, desk-bound, read-a-document task for a conservative professional audience. Dark is fully supported for late-night readers and system preference, rendered as ink-ground rather than a naive inversion.
- **Type is the identity.** Libre Caslon (Display + Text) for anything with voice; Public Sans for anything with a job. Caslon is the historical typeface of legal and government printing — contracts, statutes, official records. Public Sans is the typeface of the US federal government's design system, which is the visual world an immigration attorney already lives in. The pairing means something rather than merely looking premium.
- **One accent: oxblood.** A deep blue-red, never orange-red, never a gradient. Used at signature moments only.
- **Anti-references:** the AI-startup house style — warm cream grounds, Instrument/Newsreader serifs, purple-blue gradients, glassmorphism, robot and circuitry imagery, glowing orbs, identical three-across icon cards, everything centered. Also anti-reference: legal-industry cliché — gavels, columns, scales of justice, navy-and-gold.

### Design Principles
1. **Set it, don't decorate it.** Hierarchy comes from type size, weight, and space. If a flourish could be removed without losing meaning, remove it.
2. **Asymmetry over centering.** A left label rail against a left-aligned content column; documents sit off-axis. Centered layouts read as templated.
3. **Structure must encode something true.** Numbering appears only where the content is genuinely a sequence (the employment lifecycle). Never as decoration.
4. **The demo is the argument.** The live interview is the page's thesis, not a feature section. Everything above it exists to get the attorney into it; everything below it exists to answer what it raised.
5. **Openly artificial, visibly accountable.** Disclosure is a design element, not fine print. No synthetic faces, no human names for AI staff, no invented statistics.
